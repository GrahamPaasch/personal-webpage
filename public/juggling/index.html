Seven balls: from 35 catches to 100

My best seven-ball cascade ever is 35 catches. The goal is 100. I practice with real balls and a VR headset that shows the ideal pattern. The laptop films every session, a tracker counts and measures the runs against that same ideal, and each session becomes a short coaching video. If it works, it becomes an article for the IJA: “I took my seven-ball from 35 catches to 100 catches using AI.”

    The diagram’s math: Kalvan’s optimal cascade

    Jack Kalvan’s “Optimal Juggling” (1996) calls a pattern spatially optimal when it leaves the same room for error everywhere balls are likely to collide. In a cascade there are two such places: two balls on the same arc, near the top, one rising and one falling; and two balls on opposite arcs, where they cross in the middle. Give each ball a circular safety zone of diameter D1 (Kalvan: “adding a safety factor into the diameter of the balls”). The pattern is optimal when neighbours on one arc just graze at the top and balls on crossing arcs just graze in the middle. Every number below follows from three choices: the tempo τ, the dwell ratio r and the flight distance F.

    How this works

    1. I open the laptop page and press Start. The webcam records; I alternate real seven-ball attempts with shadow juggling in the VR headset.
    2. A ball tracker finds every attempt, counts the balls thrown, and measures peak height, width and tempo against the ideal pattern above (the same guidelines the headset draws).
    3. The pipeline cuts a coaching video under 3 minutes: the attempts, me against the ideal, and two fixes for next time.
    4. This page updates itself. Then I pick seven balls up off the floor and do it again.

    Why the counts are careful

    From the laptop on the ottoman, the top of a real seven-ball pattern leaves the frame and the catches happen behind my forearms. So the tracker counts throws per attempt, not catches. Catches on this page are either measured (once the camera can see them) or my own count, and they are labelled which.

    A flash is all seven balls thrown once and all seven caught: 7 catches.

    History

    This page started on 9 November 2026 as “50 hours of the inverted box”, a 3-ball trick I once could do. It is a synchronous pattern, siteswap (4,2x)(2x,4): on every beat one hand throws a ball straight up its own column on the outside while the other hand passes a ball straight across, flat, from hand to hand under it, and then the hands swap jobs. Seeing the ideal seven-ball pattern in VR changed the plan, and since 22 November 2026 the project is seven balls only.
