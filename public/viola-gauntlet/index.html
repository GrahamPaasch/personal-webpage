Viola Gauntlet

Seven daily practice routines for viola, one for each day of the week, rotating. Each one is a scrolling-sheet-music video with a backing track that plays along: drones to tune against, piano and strings under the scales and melodies, a light groove for the bowing work. Play along with the video; the score scrolls with a playhead and every exercise starts with two bars of count-in clicks.

Every day opens with the same core (about 7 minutes, in that day's key): open-string long tones over a drone for bow control, drone intonation on the day's scale, and perfect fifths across strings as a hand-frame check. Then comes the day's focus. Items marked stretch are deliberately past today's comfort zone: aim at them, don't force them.

Day 1 · Monday: Scales & arpeggios

Key of the day: C major / C melodic minor · 22 min · 11 exercises

Three-octave scales and the Flesch arpeggio cycle in C, slow bows first, then faster slurs. The drone and a soft string chord sit under every scale so you can hear intonation, not just feel it.

Watch on the video site Printable part (PDF) Backing track only (mp3)

  1. Open-string long tones (3 min)
    One unbroken tone per bow, in tune with the drone; middle sounding point, full even tone

    Bow control and tone on the four open strings, each tuned against a low drone an octave below. First an 8-beat bow each way swelling p to mf and back, then 12-beat bows as a messa di voce (pp, swell to f, back to pp). Today's sounding point: middle sounding point, full even tone. Soft clicks keep the beat so you can divide the bow into equal parts (a quarter of the bow per beat on the 8-beat bows).

    Stretch: 12-beat bows with no audible bow change

  2. Drone intonation (2 min)
    Each note rings with the drone - no beats; thirds low, leading note high

    A two-octave scale in half notes over a drone of the tonic and fifth, so every note can be heard as an interval against the drone. Then an interval ladder: tonic, then each scale degree in turn, then the notes borrowed from the parallel minor. Tune until the sound goes still (no beating): major thirds and sixths sit a little lower than the piano, the leading note leans up.

  3. Fifths across strings (2 min)
    One finger flat across two strings: a pure fifth means the hand frame is right

    Perfect fifths stopped by a single finger lying across two strings, every finger, every string pair, in the notes of today's key. Sustain the double stop until it is pure (the drone gives the lower note), then play it broken: lower, upper, both. A fifth that won't come pure means the finger is not square across both strings, so it diagnoses the hand frame: fix the finger's angle rather than squeezing.

  4. C major scale, 3 octaves (3 min)
    Even tone and exact shifts from C3 to C6 and back; bow speed matched to slurs

    The C major scale over three octaves (C3 to C6), five passes: whole bows one note per bow (half notes), then 2, 4 and 8 notes per bow as the rhythm doubles. Standard Flesch fingering: shift on the same finger pattern every octave (1-2-3 / 1-2-3-4, shifts after the 3rd finger going up). A C drone and a soft C major string chord sit underneath.

    Stretch: 16ths at q=80, 8 per bow, without rushing the top octave

  5. C melodic minor, 3 octaves (3 min)
    Raised 6th and 7th going up, natural going down; keep the minor third low

    C melodic minor over three octaves, same five passes as the major scale. Up: A natural and B natural; down: B flat and A flat. The minor third (E flat) and the minor sixth coming down sit low against the drone.

    Stretch: 16ths at q=80 with the top octave as clean as the bottom

  6. Arpeggio cycle (Flesch), 3 octaves (3 min)
    Six arpeggios that all start on C: frame the hand, shift on the open finger

    Carl Flesch's arpeggio cycle: six arpeggios that all begin on low C - C major, C minor, A flat major (first inversion), A minor (first inversion), F minor and F major (second inversion) - each three octaves up and back. First slurred 3 per bow, then 6 per bow. A soft string chord and low cello note give the harmony.

    Stretch: 6 per bow at this tempo; land C6 in tune every time

  7. Seventh arpeggios, 3 octaves (2 min)
    Four notes per octave: the hand frame changes shape, the shifts stay quiet

    The dominant seventh (C E G B flat) and diminished seventh (C E flat G flat A) arpeggios on C, three octaves: separate bows, 4 per bow, then 8 per bow in sixteenths.

    Stretch: 16th-note pass, 8 per bow, through C6

  8. Broken thirds, 2 octaves (1.0 min)
    Finger pairs land together; hear each third against the drone

    The C major scale in broken thirds (C-E, D-F, E-G...) up two octaves and back, slurred 2 then 4 per bow. Preparation for Wednesday's double-stop thirds.

  9. Scales on one string (1.4 min)
    A whole octave on ONE string: shifts by 1-2-3, 1-2-3-4, slow and slide-free

    One octave of C major notes on each string alone (C string from C, G string from G, D string from D, A string from A), so every note change is a shift. Use 1-2-3-1-2-3-4 style fingerings and keep the shifts silent - release finger pressure, move, then put weight back.

    Stretch: the A string octave (A4 to A5) clean, no audible slides

  10. Chromatic scale, 2 octaves (1.1 min)
    Tiny, even half steps; the fingers move like a zipper, not a slide

    Two octaves of chromatic scale from C3 to C5 and back (sharps up, flats down), 4 then 8 per bow. Half steps on the viola are wide - keep them even, and keep the hand frame still while the fingers do the work.

  11. Performance pass, with a groove (1.2 min)
    Play the scales like music: steady 16ths, big tone, locked to the bass

    The two three-octave scales once more at a brisk tempo in sixteenths, now over a light groove (pizzicato bass and off-beat piano chords): detache, then 4 and 8 per bow. This is the 'play it like it matters' pass.

    Stretch: all six passes at q=84 without a stumble

Day 2 · Tuesday: Shifting

Key of the day: D major · 23 min · 11 exercises

Shifting in D major: guide-note shifts from 1st position up to 3rd, 5th and 7th, same-finger and new-finger shifts, one-finger scales and a three-octave scale with marked shift points. Every shift target is held over a drone of that note so you can hear whether you landed, and the day ends with a short melody that climbs to 7th position.

Watch on the video site Printable part (PDF) Backing track only (mp3)

  1. Open-string long tones (3 min)
    One unbroken tone per bow, in tune with the drone; closer to the bridge: slower bow, more weight

    Bow control and tone on the four open strings, each tuned against a low drone an octave below. First an 8-beat bow each way swelling p to mf and back, then 12-beat bows held at an even pp (son file). Today's sounding point: closer to the bridge: slower bow, more weight. Soft clicks keep the beat so you can divide the bow into equal parts (a quarter of the bow per beat on the 8-beat bows).

    Stretch: 12-beat bows with no audible bow change

  2. Drone intonation (2 min)
    Each note rings with the drone - no beats; thirds low, leading note high

    A two-octave scale in half notes over a drone of the tonic and fifth, so every note can be heard as an interval against the drone. Then an interval ladder: tonic, then each scale degree in turn, then the notes borrowed from the parallel minor. Tune until the sound goes still (no beating): major thirds and sixths sit a little lower than the piano, the leading note leans up.

  3. Fifths across strings (2 min)
    One finger flat across two strings: a pure fifth means the hand frame is right

    Perfect fifths stopped by a single finger lying across two strings, every finger, every string pair, in the notes of today's key. Sustain the double stop until it is pure (the drone gives the lower note), then play it broken: lower, upper, both. A fifth that won't come pure means the finger is not square across both strings, so it diagnoses the hand frame: fix the finger's angle rather than squeezing.

  4. Guide-note shifts: 1st to 3rd (2 min)
    Slide the OLD finger to the small note, then drop the new one; hold and check

    Shifts between 1st and 3rd position on all four strings. Each string has two pairs: a same-finger shift (1 slides to 1, the glissando line shows the slide) and a new-finger shift with a guide note. The small note in parentheses is the guide: slide the finger you are already on to it, then drop the new finger on the target (Galamian's old-finger shift). Hear the guide faintly at first; once the shift feels sure, make it silent. The target is held a whole bar while the drone sounds that same note two octaves down, so you can hear at once whether you landed. The drone always changes to the next target a bar early so the pitch is in your ear before you shift. Backing: drone on the target notes; a soft solo-viola cue doubles the target only on the G- and D-string new-finger shifts (E4 and B4) as a unison check. Release finger weight while sliding, and let the arm carry the hand.

  5. Shifts to 4th and 5th position (2 min)
    Bigger shifts, same method: light finger, steady arm, land and listen

    Shifts from 1st position to 4th and 5th on the G, D and A strings: a same-finger slide first, then a new-finger shift with its guide note (small, in parentheses). Going up, the thumb travels with the hand and the elbow swings in under the viola; coming down, the thumb leads back to its 1st-position spot. The target is held a whole bar over a drone of the same note. Backing: drone on each target, plus a soft solo-viola cue on the target of every new-finger shift (F#4, D5 and A5) so you can match it in unison.

  6. Shifts to 6th position (1.4 min)
    Long slides on D and A: aim by ear, hold the target and match the drone

    Shifts from 1st position up to 6th position on the D string (to D5 and E5) and the A string (to A5 and B5), written in treble clef so the high notes sit on the staff. The target is held a whole bar over a drone of that note; a soft solo-viola cue plays the target with you on the new-finger shifts. Keep the thumb under the neck until the hand meets the body of the viola, then let it drift toward the neck heel. Above 5th position the notes are closer together: shrink the hand frame.

  7. Shifts to 7th position (1.4 min)
    The longest slides of the day: D5 and F#5 on the D string, A5 and D6 on the A

    Stretch work: shifts from 1st position to 7th on the D string (D5, F#5) and the A string (A5, D6). The top D6 is the octave check: it should ring with the low D drone. If a target is out, stay on it and adjust, then repeat the shift slowly in your head before moving on. The target is held a whole bar over a drone of that note; a soft solo-viola cue plays the target with you on the new-finger shifts. Keep the thumb under the neck until the hand meets the body of the viola, then let it drift toward the neck heel. Above 5th position the notes are closer together: shrink the hand frame.

    Stretch: 7th position: D6 on the A string in tune on the first try

  8. Same finger vs. new finger (2 min)
    Climb 1st to 5th position and back: slide on one finger, then swap fingers

    A position ladder (after Sevcik and Yost) on the D string, then the A string, through positions 1 to 5 and back. First pass: every bar is the same 1-2-3-4 shape one position higher, and the shift across the barline is a same-finger slide on finger 1. Second pass: the bar ends on finger 2 and the next bar starts on finger 1 on the SAME note (a new-finger shift by substitution); coming down it is 1 then 2. The repeated note must not change pitch or tone. Backing: a soft D-A drone and a click on every beat.

  9. One-finger scales (2 min)
    One finger slides a whole octave: the arm measures every distance

    The D major scale one octave up and back on a single string with a single finger, so every note is a shift (Flesch's one-finger scale). G string and D string with finger 1, the D string again with finger 2 (the first E sits in half position), then the A string with finger 1 up to 7th position. Slide in the slur, change position cleanly at the bow change, and notice the steps getting shorter as you climb. Backing: a soft D-A drone and a click on every beat.

    Stretch: the A-string octave on finger 1 (up to A5 in 7th position)

  10. D major, 3 octaves: shift points (2 min)
    Shift where marked (3 to 1 going up, 1 to 3 coming down); keep the slides quiet

    D major from low D (C string, 1st finger) to D6 and back. Four notes per string in 1st position (1-2-3-4) on the C, G and D strings, then on the A string 1-2-3 and two 3-to-1 shifts: up to 4th position on E5, up to 7th position on A5. Coming down, 1-to-3 shifts back to 4th position (G5) and 1st position (D5). The shift points are written under the notes; fingerings are shown in full on the first pass. Passes: one note per bow, 4 per bow, 8 per bow. A D drone and a soft D major string chord sit underneath.

    Stretch: the top octave (7th position, up to D6) in 8-per-bow slurs

  11. Melody: climbing to 7th position (3 min)
    Sing it on the viola: the shifts are part of the phrase, not gaps in it

    An original 20-bar melody in D major, played twice. It starts in 1st position, moves to 3rd (bar 5), 5th (bar 10) and touches 7th position at the climax (bars 13-14, up to D6), then settles back to 3rd. Positions are marked over the notes. Use the shifts expressively - a slight, unhurried slide on the long shifts is fine - but land in tune. The second time, add more vibrato and longer lines. Backing: piano broken chords and a soft string pad; nothing doubles the melody.

    Stretch: bars 13-14: the shift to 7th position and a full, in-tune D6

Day 3 · Wednesday: Double stops

Key of the day: G major (cadences also in D) · 21 min · 11 exercises

Double stops in G: open-string pairs first, then the scale in thirds, sixths and octaves, slow and sustained before they move. A low G and D drone sits under the scales so every interval can be tuned until it rings, and the week's chord work closes with I-IV-V-I cadences in G and D.

  1. Open-string long tones (3 min)
    One unbroken tone per bow, in tune with the drone; halfway point, let the string ring after each bow

    Bow control and tone on the four open strings, each tuned against a low drone an octave below. First an 8-beat bow each way swelling p to mf and back, then 12-beat bows as a messa di voce (pp, swell to f, back to pp). Today's sounding point: halfway point, let the string ring after each bow. Soft clicks keep the beat so you can divide the bow into equal parts (a quarter of the bow per beat on the 8-beat bows).

    Stretch: 12-beat bows with no audible bow change

  2. Drone intonation (2 min)
    Each note rings with the drone - no beats; thirds low, leading note high

    A two-octave scale in half notes over a drone of the tonic and fifth, so every note can be heard as an interval against the drone. Then an interval ladder: tonic, then each scale degree in turn, then the notes borrowed from the parallel minor. Tune until the sound goes still (no beating): major thirds and sixths sit a little lower than the piano, the leading note leans up.

  3. Fifths across strings (2 min)
    One finger flat across two strings: a pure fifth means the hand frame is right

    Perfect fifths stopped by a single finger lying across two strings, every finger, every string pair, in the notes of today's key. Sustain the double stop until it is pure (the drone gives the lower note), then play it broken: lower, upper, both. A fifth that won't come pure means the finger is not square across both strings, so it diagnoses the hand frame: fix the finger's angle rather than squeezing.

  4. Double stops with an open string (2 min)
    The open string is your drone: tune each stopped note until the pair rings

    Sevcik-style double stops against an open string on each string pair (C-G, G-D, D-A). First the stopped note sits ABOVE the lower open string (fifth, major sixth, octave), then BELOW the upper open string (fourth, minor third). The open string cannot be out of tune, so it acts as a built-in drone: move only the stopped finger until the double stop stops beating and the open string rings in sympathy. Bow weight shared equally between both strings; the bow sits on the plane between them. First pass in half notes with fingerings, second pass in quarters, 2 per bow. A low cello drone doubles the lower open string an octave down.

  5. Finger pairs (Sevcik op. 9) (2 min)
    Two fingers move as one unit: both notes land together, in tune, no gap

    Before the scales, the finger pairs that build them, Sevcik op. 9 style: two neighbouring double stops alternated in one position - thirds fingered 3-1 and 4-2, sixths fingered 1-2 and 2-3 - first sustained, then in quarters and eighths, slurred. Lift and drop each pair of fingers together as one block from the base knuckles; don't let the lower finger arrive first. Keep the hand frame still: the thumb stays put while the fingers change. Soft G and D drone underneath.

  6. G major in thirds, 2 octaves (2 min)
    Major thirds low, minor thirds high: every third rings against the G drone

    The G major scale in thirds, two octaves up and back, with the standard Flesch fingering: alternate 4-2 and 3-1 and shift every two thirds (4-2 to 3-1 moves the hand up a position). First pass sustained, one third per bow, fingerings printed; second pass slurred two per bow; then one octave in a long-short rhythm. Tune the lower note first, then place the upper note to it: major thirds sit narrow (lower than the piano), minor thirds wide. Shift with both fingers light, then lean in. The low G and D drone shows when a third is true - it stops beating. The top octave (5th-7th position on the D and A strings) is today's stretch.

    Stretch: the top octave (5th-7th position) as clean as the bottom

  7. G major in sixths (2 min)
    Neighbouring fingers on neighbouring strings: major sixths low, minor high

    The G major scale in sixths from G3 up to the sixth B4-G5 and back. In first position the fingering walks across the strings 0-1, 1-2, 2-3, 3-4 (G and D strings, then D and A); the top two sixths move to 4th position, 1-2 and 2-3. A sixth on the viola is two neighbouring fingers on neighbouring strings, so the finger pair barely changes shape - tune the lower note, let the upper finger fall beside it. Major sixths (G-E, D-B) sit low, minor sixths (B-G, E-C) wide. Sustained, then slurred 2, then in a long-short rhythm over the G and D drone.

  8. G major in octaves (1.4 min)
    The hand frame again: 1 and 4 a fixed octave apart, moved as one block

    One octave of G major in octaves. Open G with 3rd finger on the D string first, then fingers 1 and 4 shifting a position at a time (A, B, C on the G and D strings), open D with 3 on the A string, and 1-4 again for E, F sharp, G on the D and A strings. The 1st-4th finger octave IS the hand frame: set it once, then move the whole frame up and down the string without letting it open or close. Listen to the upper note: an octave in tune sounds like one fat note. Sustained, slurred 2, then eighths 4 per bow (the stretch) over the G and D drone.

    Stretch: the eighth-note pass with every octave still pure

  9. Three-note chords: I-IV-V-I (1.5 min)
    Ring three strings at once: bow near the frog, weight into the middle string

    Three-note chords in the simplest real music there is: the I-IV-V-I cadence in G, then in D. Each chord is first broken across the strings (bass, middle, top, middle, slurred) so you can check every note, then played together as a held chord, all down-bow. For a three-note chord, start close to the frog, aim the bow at the middle string and let the weight hold the outer two; don't crush. Finger 1 lies flat across two strings for the D and A chords - the same fifth as in the core exercise. The last lines play both cadences in quarter-note chords and close on G. A soft string pad and low cello root play the same chords underneath.

  10. Fingered octaves (stretch) (1.3 min)
    Octaves without sliding: 1-3, 2-4, and the hand opens instead of shifting

    The stretch item: fingered octaves (Flesch), G-A-B-C on the D and A strings. Instead of 1-4 shifting every note, alternate 1-3 and 2-4: the 3rd finger reaches out to where the 4th normally sits, and the hand moves only once (from 2-4 in 3rd position to 1-3 in 5th). It opens the hand from the base knuckles - warm up first, never force the stretch, and stop if anything aches. Whole notes, then half notes, then slurred 2. Treble clef for the top notes. Soft G and D drone.

    Stretch: the whole exercise: fingered octaves are a long-term target

  11. Chorale in thirds and sixths (2 min)
    Make double stops music: one voice sings on top, the other supports it

    A short original chorale in G, all in first position, moving between the thirds and sixths you just practised (fingerings printed on the first time through). Bring out the top voice a little - it's the tune - and keep the lower voice smooth underneath. Connect the double stops with no gap at the bow change. Played twice: first p and plain, then mf with a little vibrato on both notes. A soft string pad and cello bass play the harmony under you.

Day 4 · Thursday: Bowing

Key of the day: F major · 23 min · 12 exercises

One short F major figure played with a different bow stroke each time: detache, legato, martele, hooked rhythms, colle, string crossings, spiccato and sautille. The left hand stays easy so the whole day is about the right arm, over a light groove.

Watch on the video site Printable part (PDF) Backing track only (mp3)

  1. Open-string long tones (3 min)
    One unbroken tone per bow, in tune with the drone; near the fingerboard: light and fast, then back to the middle

    Bow control and tone on the four open strings, each tuned against a low drone an octave below. First an 8-beat bow each way swelling p to mf and back, then 12-beat bows held at an even pp (son file). Today's sounding point: near the fingerboard: light and fast, then back to the middle. Soft clicks keep the beat so you can divide the bow into equal parts (a quarter of the bow per beat on the 8-beat bows).

    Stretch: 12-beat bows with no audible bow change

  2. Drone intonation (2 min)
    Each note rings with the drone - no beats; thirds low, leading note high

    A two-octave scale in half notes over a drone of the tonic and fifth, so every note can be heard as an interval against the drone. Then an interval ladder: tonic, then each scale degree in turn, then the notes borrowed from the parallel minor. Tune until the sound goes still (no beating): major thirds and sixths sit a little lower than the piano, the leading note leans up.

  3. Fifths across strings (2 min)
    One finger flat across two strings: a pure fifth means the hand frame is right

    Perfect fifths stopped by a single finger lying across two strings, every finger, every string pair, in the notes of today's key. Sustain the double stop until it is pure (the drone gives the lower note), then play it broken: lower, upper, both. A fifth that won't come pure means the finger is not square across both strings, so it diagnoses the hand frame: fix the finger's angle rather than squeezing.

  4. Detache: middle and whole bow (3 min)
    One note per bow, no gaps, no accents: the bow changes are invisible

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. The day's figure: eight bars of F major sixteenths in first position (F, B flat, C7, F, then D minor, G minor, C7, F), played three ways. First detache in the middle of the bow, a few inches of bow per note, smooth and connected. Then grand detache in eighths using the whole bow for every note - full speed, even sound from frog to tip, the arm opening and closing from the elbow. Finally detache again in the upper half, lighter. Keep the sounding point steady and make every bow change sound the same in both directions. A light groove (pizzicato bass and off-beat piano) keeps the pulse.

  5. Legato slurs: 4, 8 and 16 (2 min)
    Long slurs with an even bow speed; string crossings rolled, not bumped

    The same figure slurred 4, then 8, then 16 notes per bow (a whole bar in one bow). Divide the bow evenly over the slur so the last notes are not squeezed, and roll the arm level smoothly between strings so the crossings sound like part of the line. Only the left hand articulates the notes - the fingers drop firmly so each note speaks under the slur. A soft string chord sits under it.

    Stretch: 16 notes per bow at q=60 with full tone to the end of each bow

  6. Martele: bite and stop (2 min)
    Pinch the string, release with speed, stop: clean silence between notes

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. Martele (marked, hammered stroke). In the upper half: set the bow on the string with weight (index finger), release it with a fast stroke that starts with a clean consonant, then stop the bow on the string - the silence between notes is part of the stroke. First in eighths through the whole figure, then a smaller martele in sixteenths in the middle of the bow on the first phrase. No crunch at the start: the bite comes from pressure released, not pressed harder.

    Stretch: the 16th-note martele at q=60, each stroke still stopped

  7. Hooked bowing, dotted rhythms (2 min)
    Dotted 8th and 16th in the same bow; the short note stays short and clear

    The figure in dotted rhythms (dotted eighth + sixteenth). First with separate bows, which teaches bow distribution: slow bow on the long note, fast and light on the short one so you don't drift to the tip. Then hooked: the dotted eighth and the sixteenth in the SAME bow direction with a small stop between them (down-down, up-up). Keep the rhythm crisp - the sixteenth is late and short, never a lazy triplet.

  8. Colle (optional quick drill) (0.9 min)
    At the frog: a tiny pinched click, then lift the bow off the string

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. Colle (glued): optional, about a minute. At the frog, place the bow on the string, pinch it with the fingers so the string 'catches', release with a tiny fast finger-and-wrist stroke, and lift off in the rest. Alternating bows first, then all down-bows with a lift and reset between notes. This is the finger action behind every clean bow start and behind spiccato later today.

  9. String crossings: bariolage (2 min)
    Fingered note against the open string: tiny level changes, from wrist and fingers

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. Bariolage: a fingered note on the lower string alternating with the open string above it, first D against open A, then G-string notes against open D. Separate bows first (small strokes in the middle, the forearm and wrist making the crossing), then slurred, where only the wrist and fingers rock the bow - the arm stays at a level between the two strings. Open strings ring; keep them as loud as the fingered notes, not louder. The harmony is chosen so the open string always belongs to the chord.

    Stretch: the slurred 8s across G-D with no bumps on the open D

  10. Spiccato, lower half (2 min)
    Off the string: each note is a small dropped arc, clear and even

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. Spiccato in the lower half of the bow (find the spot a little below the balance point where the bow bounces most easily). The arm makes small U-shaped arcs: the bow drops into the string and leaves it again on every note. First in eighths, then in sixteenths with smaller, lower arcs - the faster it goes, the closer the bow stays to the string. Listen for a clear 'tock' with pitch, not a scratch or a splash.

    Stretch: spiccato 16ths at q=72 through the whole figure

  11. Sautille, brisk (0.9 min)
    Fast short detache at the balance point: the stick bounces, you just steer

    Cues: M = middle of the bow, WB = whole bow, UH = upper half, LH = lower half, Fr = at the frog. Sautille: a fast detache in the middle of the bow (at or just above the balance point) where the hair stays close to the string and the stick's own spring makes it bounce. Don't throw the bow - play a small fast detache with a loose hand, tilt the stick slightly and let the bounce appear. Start each pass on the string if it doesn't bounce yet. This is a stretch tempo today: if it falls apart, play the passes on the string at q=100 and keep the hand loose.

    Stretch: the whole exercise: sautille 16ths at q=100

  12. Mixed bowings (Kreutzer 2 style) (2 min)
    Uneven groupings with even sound: plan where the bow is before each group

    The 'performance pass': the figure with the classic Kreutzer No. 2 bowing variants. 2 slurred + 2 separate, 1 separate + 3 slurred, 3 slurred + 1 separate. These groupings pull the bow toward one end: use more bow (faster) for the slurs and less for the separate notes so you stay in the middle. Play it like music, with a big tone, over the groove.

    Stretch: all three patterns at q=66 without drifting to the frog or tip

Day 5 · Friday: Vibrato & tone

Key of the day: E-flat major · 24 min · 10 exercises

Tone first: sounding point, bow speed and a dynamic ladder over an E-flat drone, then vibrato practised as measured rhythm (2, 3, 4 and 6 per beat) on every finger. It ends with an original slow cantabile in E-flat where the vibrato never stops and the phrases have room to breathe.

Watch on the video site Printable part (PDF) Backing track only (mp3)

  1. Open-string long tones (3 min)
    One unbroken tone per bow, in tune with the drone; contact point steady, watch the bow stay parallel to the bridge

    Bow control and tone on the four open strings, each tuned against a low drone an octave below. First an 8-beat bow each way swelling p to mf and back, then 12-beat bows as a messa di voce (pp, swell to f, back to pp). Today's sounding point: contact point steady, watch the bow stay parallel to the bridge. Soft clicks keep the beat so you can divide the bow into equal parts (a quarter of the bow per beat on the 8-beat bows).

    Stretch: 12-beat bows with no audible bow change

  2. Drone intonation (2 min)
    Each note rings with the drone - no beats; thirds low, leading note high

    A two-octave scale in half notes over a drone of the tonic and fifth, so every note can be heard as an interval against the drone. Then an interval ladder: tonic, then each scale degree in turn, then the notes borrowed from the parallel minor. Tune until the sound goes still (no beating): major thirds and sixths sit a little lower than the piano, the leading note leans up.

  3. Fifths across strings (2 min)
    One finger flat across two strings: a pure fifth means the hand frame is right

    Perfect fifths stopped by a single finger lying across two strings, every finger, every string pair, in the notes of today's key. Sustain the double stop until it is pure (the drone gives the lower note), then play it broken: lower, upper, both. A fifth that won't come pure means the finger is not square across both strings, so it diagnoses the hand frame: fix the finger's angle rather than squeezing.

  4. Sounding point & bow speed (2 min)
    Move toward the bridge to get louder: slower bow, more weight, same clean tone

    Tone is made by three things together: bow speed, bow weight and where the bow touches the string (the sounding point). On four E-flat-chord notes, one per string (E-flat on the C string and B-flat on the G string with the 2nd finger, G on the D string with the 3rd, high E-flat on the A string with the 4th), first sweep the bow from over the fingerboard (sul tasto) to near the bridge while you crescendo pp to f, and back again on the up bow. Then hold each sounding point for a whole bow: tasto (fast, light bow, airy), middle, near the bridge (slow, heavy bow, rich - but stop short of the glassy ponticello squeak), middle again. Near the bridge the string needs slow bow and weight; near the fingerboard it needs speed and almost no weight. The soft clicks mark each beat: use a quarter of the bow per beat. An E-flat drone (cello) sits underneath. Vibrato on, but the bow is the subject.

  5. Dynamic ladder, pp to ff (2 min)
    Every bow one dynamic step louder (then softer), with the tone staying centred

    A ladder of dynamics on one long note, one step per 4-beat bow: pp, p, mp, mf, f, ff and back down. First on G (D string, 3rd finger), then the stretch: E-flat on the C string from ppp to fff and back. Make each step clearly different from the last - most players only really have three dynamics. Get louder by moving the bow nearer the bridge and adding weight, softer by moving toward the fingerboard and lightening, not just by speeding up or slowing down the bow. Let the vibrato grow with the dynamic. The E-flat drone stays at one level so you can hear how far you travel.

    Stretch: the C-string pass, ppp to fff and back, with a ppp that still sings

  6. Measured vibrato: 2 and 3 per beat (3 min)
    Rock from the note to just below and back, exactly in time with the clicks

    Vibrato practised as rhythm. Each note is held for one whole bow, and the number over it is the speed of the vibrato: '2 per beat' means two complete rocks per click (like eighth notes), '3 per beat' three (like triplets). One rock = from the note down to slightly below it and back up to the note - the pitch you hear is the top of the vibrato, never above it. These are not separate notes: one finger, one pitch, the rhythm is only the motion. Every finger (1-2-3-4) on every string in first position in E-flat (no open strings - you can't vibrate those). Keep the motion even, wide enough to hear, and the thumb soft; the vibrato does not stop at the bow change. The drone plays each note an octave lower so you can hear the vibrato stay at or below pitch. Soft clicks on every beat at q=60.

  7. Measured vibrato: 4 and 6 per beat (2 min)
    Faster rocking, still even: 4 per beat, then 6 per beat (stretch)

    The same drill one gear faster: each note gets one bow at 4 rocks per beat (like sixteenths), then one bow at 6 per beat (like sextuplets) - G, D and A strings, fingers 1-4. The written number is the speed of the rocking from the note to just below and back, not separate notes. At 6 per beat the motion gets smaller; that's fine, but it must stay even and loose (if the thumb squeezes or the sound starts to bleat, drop back to 4). It ends with a ladder on one G (D string, 3rd finger): 2, 3, 4, 6, 4 per beat, then a bow of your own natural vibrato - which is usually somewhere between 4 and 6. The drone plays each note an octave lower; soft clicks on every beat at q=60.

    Stretch: 6 per beat (sextuplet speed), even and relaxed, on the 4th finger too

  8. Continuous vibrato (2 min)
    The vibrato keeps going through every finger change and bow change

    A common fault is vibrato that switches off for a moment every time a finger changes, so each note starts 'naked'. Here the vibrato motion never stops: it carries through the finger change and the bow change, like a singer's voice. First a two-octave E-flat major scale in half notes, slurred 2 per bow; use the 4th finger instead of the open G and D strings so every note can be vibrated. Then slow finger 'trills' (1-2, 2-3, 3-4 on the D, A, G and C strings) slurred 4 per bow: the new finger drops into a hand that is already vibrating. Each group ends with a held note. Quiet E-flat drone.

  9. Vibrato width & dynamics (2 min)
    pp = narrow and quick, ff = wide and rich; the vibrato follows the dynamic

    Vibrato is part of the dynamic, not decoration on top of it. On five notes of the E-flat chord, one per bow: pp with a narrow, quick shimmer; mf medium; ff wide and rich (wide must not mean slow - keep it alive); then a diminuendo where the vibrato narrows with the sound. Width comes from the forearm/hand swing, not from pressing the string harder. The softest notes are where vibrato is most often lost; keep it going, just smaller. E-flat drone underneath, soft click each bar.

    Stretch: the ff on the 4th-finger E-flat: wide vibrato with no pressing or bleat

  10. Cantabile in E-flat (original) (3 min)
    Sing it: unbroken vibrato, long bows, and let every rest breathe

    An original 20-bar slow melody in E-flat major that puts the day together: five four-bar phrases, each ending on a long note and a rest so the music can breathe. Phrase 1 asks a question (ends on the dominant), phrase 2 answers, phrase 3 climbs the A string and cadences deceptively on C minor, phrase 4 is the climax - hold the high E-flat through the change of chord and let it resolve down to D - and a low, quiet coda brings it home. Played twice: first to find the line, then with more vibrato and more bow. Use what you practised: the bow nearer the bridge for the f, vibrato that never stops between notes, wider at the climax and narrower in the coda. Take the rests as real breaths, not as gaps. A piano plays broken chords and a soft string pad holds the harmony; neither plays your melody.
