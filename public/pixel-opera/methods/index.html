Opera methods: the same 38 seconds, six ways

Mozart, Le nozze di Figaro, Act I: the military verse of "Non più andrai" (Figaro marches Cherubino through the glories of army life: moustache, tight tunic, musket, helmet, plenty of honour and little pay, a march through the mud). Same orchestra and sung line, same captions and timings, 1080p, in six different visual methods, each pushed as far as it would go in one session, so the method can be judged head to head: how it looks, what it costs to scale to a whole opera, and how likely the characters are to drift.

 

Honest pick

Painted storybook puppets (method 3). It has the painted-illustration look of the stills but moves on the music, the characters cannot drift because they are drawn once, and a new character costs about twenty minutes of generation and cutting. Shadow theatre is the runner-up: nearly free to scale and the most elegant frame, but faceless. The illustrated stills are gorgeous and held consistency better than expected, yet they do not act. The vector cartoon and the 3D stage are the cheapest to re-render and the most controllable, but both look like what they are: code.

1. Vector cartoon in code (Remotion)

Two rigged SVG puppets with tracking eyes, syllable-driven mouths and chalk-outline 'ghost props' (moustache, musket, helmet, coin) in a flat vector room, with camera punch-ins and black-band opera surtitles.

Tooling
Remotion 4 (React, TypeScript) rendered headless in Chrome on CPU; every character, set piece and gag is code (SVG + keyframe tracks); ffmpeg
Scaling cost
Render is real-time on CPU, no GPU. Authoring is the cost: ~1 h per character rig, ~40 min per room, 20-40 min of blocking per minute of music if every line gets a gag; a whole opera is roughly 60-100 agent-hours of blocking plus 10-15 h of assets.
Drift risk
Low (puppets are code; they cannot drift. The risk is stiffness and sameness instead.)
Made how
No generated assets. Drawn once as parametrised SVG, blocked line by line on the beat grid, one polish round (ink outlines, paper grain, coat tails, mitten hands).
Verdict
Most gags per second and the clearest faces, but even with ink outlines and paper grain it reads as clip art next to the painted methods.

2. Shadow-puppet theatre (paper cutouts)

Lace-filigree black cut-outs with jointed limbs on a warm back-lit paper screen inside a cut-paper proscenium; Figaro's hand lands on the boy's lip, waist, chin and head by inverse kinematics.

Tooling
Silhouette pieces generated once on the Mac (FLUX.2 klein, 20 pieces), thresholded to 1-bit masks; rig, choreography and compositing in Python + Pillow + numpy on CPU
Scaling cost
Art is nearly free: one generated silhouette plus ~1 h of cutting and pivots per character, set pieces in minutes, no per-shot art. Choreography is the cost (~150 keyframes for 38 s); a pose/gag macro library would bring a whole opera to ~1-2 agent-hours per minute. Render ~6 min per minute of video on CPU.
Drift risk
Low (one fixed cut-out per character). The limit is expression: no faces, poses must read in profile.
Made how
Generated silhouettes cut into head, torso, legs and two-segment arms with joint discs; keyframed with easing, breathing and per-syllable head nods; props keyed to hands.
Verdict
The most elegant frame for the least art risk; the gags read in profile, but there are no faces, so the comedy is all in the hands.

3. Painted storybook puppets

Gouache-and-ink storybook illustrations generated once (two bodies, two expression-head sheets, props, an empty room), cut into 12-bone jointed paper puppets and animated over the painted room with parallax foreground pieces and a parchment caption band.

Tooling
FLUX.2 klein 4B via mflux on the Mac Studio for the source art (15 images); rig, forward-kinematic puppets, staging, camera and compositing in Python + Pillow + numpy + scipy on CPU
Scaling cost
Art is fixed cost: ~20 min per character (four generations plus cutting and bones), ~10 min per set, no per-shot generation. Staging is the cost: 1-1.5 h of keyframing per minute for gag-dense scenes, 15-20 min per minute for stand-and-sing; render 3 min per minute. Whole opera ≈ 100-150 h of staging, under 10 h of render.
Drift risk
Low (each character is drawn exactly once; only the body-sheet vs head-sheet join can mismatch, and both come from the same prompt and style).
Made how
Body sheets, head sheets and props generated once; white background stripped; pixels assigned to the nearest bone with round joint overlaps; keyframed gags, prop drop-ins, syllable head accents, parallax push-ins; one polish round (feet clear of the band, matched Cherubino head style, tighter framing).
Verdict
The look of the stills with the motion of the puppets: painted, consistent, funny. The winner on this excerpt.

4. 3D low-poly stage (three.js)

A toy theatre in 3D: flat-shaded low-poly Figaro and Cherubino on a proscenium stage with velvet curtains, footlights, a painted backdrop and real props, a warm key spot with soft shadows and a moving camera.

Tooling
three.js 0.187 in headless Chrome driven by puppeteer, software GL (SwiftShader) on CPU, HTML/CSS surtitle overlay captured in the same screenshot; ffmpeg
Scaling cost
Render ~4 min per minute of video on CPU. Characters ~1 h each, sets 1-2 h; blocking 2-3 h per minute at this gag density until a gesture library exists (then 20-40 min per minute). Any camera angle and any lighting come free once the scene exists.
Drift risk
Low (characters are code, identical in every frame, from any angle). The risk is a samey, toy-like look and mitten hands.
Made how
No assets at all: stage, props and two rigged characters from primitives; a hand-written pose function keyed to the caption times; nine eased camera moves.
Verdict
Real staging, lighting and camera for free once the scene exists, but toy-like characters with mitten hands; strong for ensembles and crowd scenes.

5. Illustrated stills + motion

Ten gouache-and-ink storybook stills, one per gag, each generated with the SAME reference sheet and room fed to the image model, then Ken Burns moves, cross-fades on the caption times, punch-in pops on the three 'poco contante's and caption cards.

Tooling
FLUX.2 klein 4B edit (multi-reference) via mflux on the Mac Studio, 2 seeds per shot picked by eye; motion, fades and captions in Python + numpy + Pillow; ffmpeg
Scaling cost
About 25-30 min of prompting and picking per minute of video plus ~15 Mac-minutes per minute unattended (two seeds per shot); a whole opera is ~2,200 stills, ~37 Mac-hours. Render is seconds. No rigging, no choreography, but no real motion either.
Drift risk
Medium (4 of 20 generations drifted: one lost Cherubino, one doubled the musket, moustache and coat length vary; costumes, hair and room held). Every shot is a new roll of the dice.
Made how
One reference sheet (both characters) and one room image chosen from several seeds; every shot generated from them with a gag prompt; 5% paper-edge crop, 2x upscale for subpixel-smooth moves.
Verdict
The most beautiful single frames here, and the reference sheet held better than expected, but it is a slideshow: nothing moves on the beat.

6. Pixel art, round 2 (baseline)

The approved art-round-2 look: our own 32-colour palette, hand-drawn 32x48 sprites and a tiled room at 256x224, scaled 4x, with the blue RPG text box. Same timeline as the full aria, trimmed to the excerpt.

Tooling
Python + Pillow; sprites and tiles authored as palette-indexed grids in code (render_art.py, art2_*.py)
Scaling cost
Render is seconds per minute. Drawing is the cost: 2-4 h per character (idle, walk, gestures in three views) and 1-2 h per room; gestures are limited to the drawn frames, so comedy stays coarse.
Drift risk
Low (fixed sprite sheets).
Made how
Rendered from the existing art-2 timeline and cut to the excerpt; nothing new was drawn for the bake-off.
Verdict
Coherent but small and generic; the method that started this. Included so the others can be judged against it.
